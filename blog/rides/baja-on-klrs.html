Baja on KLRs

January 1, 2026 · Kawasaki KLR 650

Why this ride?

Ellie and I wanted to take advantage of the break that happens around Christmas, so between Christmas and New Year's we went down to Baja California Sur. We picked up two KLR 650s in Cabo and rode around the peninsula, following a lot of the published Baja 1000 route. We were curious whether we'd be into rally racing, and this seemed like a good way to test the waters.

Little did we know, the KLR 650 was absolutely not the motorcycle we should have picked for this. It's a bit too heavy, and not a true dual-sport. We stayed on dirt roads as much as we could, which was easy, because there are only a handful of well-developed paved roads down there. That also meant we got to see a really incredible, rare side of Baja and the people who choose to live there.

Day 1: Cabo up the East Cape to La Paz

On the ride up toward La Paz we found a town with a ton of windsurfing. It turned out to be an expat community, and it made for a great lunch stop.

We made it to La Paz just in time for dinner, a shower, and crashing.

Day 2: San Evaristo and the Sierra de la Giganta

From La Paz up the coastline to San Evaristo was some of the most beautiful geography, geology, whatever, I have ever seen. It was truly remarkable. The water was incredibly blue, and the rocks were all reds and greens and blues, full of minerals, making the most stunning landscape.

We passed a mining town that, surprisingly, had white Monster in stock, even though it was very hard to find anywhere else in the country (the countryside, at least). We saw a bunch of cool industrial facilities and some ruins along the way. Some of the riding through here was genuinely very challenging.

San Evaristo has a giant statue of a fisherman up at the top, but the town itself is tiny.

We probably wouldn't have made it on fuel if it weren't for a very kind man who took US$20 for five gallons of gas between the two of us. He even gave us a piece of marble from the local mine as a souvenir. We didn't speak Spanish and he didn't speak English, but he was so supportive, with lots of thumbs up and encouragement. He was also incredibly old and mumbled to himself a lot the whole time I was filling up.

From San Evaristo over the mountains was, again, some incredibly challenging terrain, especially on a heavy 650. Along the way we passed several cars that had caught fire and burned down to the frame. We also dropped the bikes several times. Eventually, though, we made it to the other side and found a nice taco spot where we could relax a little before trying to find a hotel.

Everything we did, we did in the moment. We never booked a hotel until that night, because we didn't know where we'd end up. We met so many people who were curious about our adventure. It seems they don't usually see women riding motorcycles through Baja on their own, and they really wanted to hear where we were coming from and why we were there.

[Dictate: Las Pocitas and signing the wall]

Also very cool: that night we saw a rocket launch from Vandenberg, all the way from where we were in Baja. That was neat.

Day 3: El Estero and around to the Pacific

On day 3, the plan was to follow the Baja 1000 route again and go all the way to the water on the Pacific coast. Down there it got more and more sparse, in people and in infrastructure. We went a very long way with no roads and very little sign of what roads there were. In a lot of spots our GPS said there should be a road, but the actual road went a different direction, and we just had to figure it out.

Eventually we made it to the coastline, but on the wrong side of the river. We were hoping it was just a small inland lake that got cut off at the beach so we could ride across. When we got there, we realized it actually went all the way out to the water, even though none of the maps showed that. It must have been the time of year. So we were stuck on the wrong side, without enough fuel to go all the way around and down to where we wanted to be.

By then we'd spent so many hours riding through sand on heavy bikes that we decided to give up, head back to the highway, and make our way down. That was absolutely the right call, because we were still riding until very late after dark, and I started falling asleep on the bike. That meant it was time to pull over and get a room wherever we could.

We got very lucky: the hotel we stopped at was in a small town with an expat community. Before bed we heard a lot of noise and partying coming from a local bar, so we went to check it out. We met a lot of people who were so excited to meet us and hear our story. I got talked into riding a mechanical bull, which was hilarious, because I was exhausted from riding a different kind of mechanical bull all day, every day, for the last few days. I killed it, and had everybody cheering until they got bored of watching me not fall off.

Day 4: Back to Cabo

From there we didn't have far to go, but it was our last day, so we had to wake up early, head down to Cabo, drop off the bikes, and catch our flight.

In the end, this was probably the most fun I've ever had on a motorcycle adventure, largely because I was doing it with the best riding partner I could ask for. We really pushed ourselves: our riding, our survival skills, and our social skills. I think it set the tone for the adventures I want to do next. Now I know I'm capable of so much more than I ever thought I was.
