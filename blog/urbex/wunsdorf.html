Wünsdorf

August 16, 2025 · Wünsdorf, Brandenburg, Germany

Approximate area only. Exact locations aren't shared.

Wünsdorf was basically a secret Soviet city. Starting in the 1930s it was the German army's high command, with huge bunkers named Maybach and Zeppelin. Zeppelin was one of the biggest communications centers of WWII, sending orders all over Europe right up until April 1945.

Then the Soviets moved in and made it the headquarters of all their forces in East Germany. Germans called it the “forbidden city” or “little Moscow”: up to 50,000–75,000 Soviets lived here, walled off from the roughly 2,700 Germans in town, with a train that ran straight to Moscow every single day until 1994.

The crown jewel is the House of Officers. It started out as the army's sports school, and German athletes trained here for the 1936 Olympics. After the war it became the Soviet officers' club, with a theater where the gym used to be, a rebuilt pool, and ballrooms.

I went with Ellie. The original Kaiser-era building, where all the really cool stuff is (the grand ballrooms, the theater, the pool), was locked up very, very tight. Security here is no joke.

But we did get into the Soviet annex, which honestly was one of the highlights. Once the Soviets made Wünsdorf their headquarters, they didn't just move into the old German buildings. They bolted on their own: curving walls of glass, columns, a red bar room, and that big geometric mosaic on the outside. I tend to find Soviet architecture way more compelling than the old Kaiser stuff anyway. The neo-baroque buildings are pretty, but the Soviet additions have so much more personality, and the iconography is incredible.

My favorite find was a giant map still hanging on a wall in the basement. It's a Soviet map of the Battle of Berlin, April 16 to May 8, 1945: front lines drawn on April 16, April 25 and May 2, with red arrows for every Soviet attack. Zhukov's 1st Belorussian Front comes in from the east across the Oder and the Seelow Heights, Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front sweeps up from the south, and the two meet west of Berlin at Ketzin on April 25 to close the ring. “200,000 people” is written twice: once for everyone trapped in Berlin, once for the German 9th Army caught in the pocket at Halbe.

And right there on it is Zossen, the next town over, with the 3rd Guards Tank Army's arrow running straight past it. That tank army took Wünsdorf around April 21, 1945. So this was a map of how they got the very building it was hanging in. It probably lived in a classroom or briefing room, part of the political education every Soviet officer here got: this is why we're here, and this is what it cost. Some modern visitor has since sprayed a “666” on it and circled Falkensee, for reasons I can't explain.

And then Lenin. He's still out front, a giant on a red granite plinth, staring down the old sports field with the clock tower behind him. We never cracked the main building, and after a close call with security we took that as our sign to get out of there. We got into the annex, and we saw Lenin. We're calling it a success.
